The First Birthday
Smash Cake Guide

A tiny cake, a baby, and roughly ninety seconds of chaos worth photographing. Here's how to set it up so it actually goes well.

The smash cake is now a fixture of first birthdays, and it's genuinely fun — but it goes better with a little planning. Here's what matters.

What it actually is

A small cake made for one baby to destroy with their hands, while everyone photographs it. It's separate from the cake the guests eat, which is why the standard package pairs a small smash cake with matching cupcakes for everyone else. That set starts at $55.

Size

Small. A 4- to 6-inch round is plenty — it should be reachable and tippable by someone with the reach of a one-year-old. A large smash cake is harder for a baby to engage with and looks oversized in photos next to them.

Flavors that work for a one-year-old

Keep it mild and simple. Vanilla, funfetti, and banana bread all go over well. Chocolate photographs beautifully but is messier and stains outfits and backdrops more aggressively — worth deciding on purpose rather than by accident.

Talk to your pediatrician about anything you're unsure of, and flag any allergies in your order. Everything is baked in a kitchen that also handles wheat, eggs, dairy, soy, tree nuts, and peanuts, so if allergies are a concern for your family, say so up front.

On frosting: a lot of parents ask for "less sweet." It's a reasonable instinct, and it's worth mentioning when you order — but expect that a cake designed to be smashed and licked is still a cake. Most babies take a few bites and mostly play with it.

Timing on the day

This is the part people get wrong. Aim for a window when the baby is fed but not full, and rested but not near a nap. A hungry baby gets frustrated; a stuffed one is uninterested; a tired one cries on camera. Mid-morning or early afternoon after a nap tends to be the sweet spot.

Do the smash before the party gets loud and crowded if you can. A room of twenty people singing at a one-year-old is a very common reason the whole thing ends in tears.

Setting up the shot

  • Put something down. A splat mat, drop cloth, or old sheet. If you're outside, grass makes cleanup trivial.
  • High chair or floor, both work. The floor gives better angles; a high chair contains the damage.
  • Watch your background. A plain wall or a simple backdrop beats a cluttered kitchen in every photo you'll keep.
  • Shoot from the baby's level, not standing over them. It's the difference between a snapshot and a portrait.
  • Have wipes and a change of clothes ready before you start, not after.

If your baby doesn't smash it

Plenty don't. Some babies stare at it, some pat it delicately, some are appalled by the texture. That's a completely normal outcome and often makes better photos than the mess. Don't push it — the pictures of a confused baby politely examining a cake are the ones families end up framing.

Ordering

Custom cakes need about a week of lead time, so book once you've settled on a date. Bring a theme or an inspiration photo if you have one — bee cakes, jungle animals, rainbows, florals — and it can be matched to whatever the party décor is doing.
